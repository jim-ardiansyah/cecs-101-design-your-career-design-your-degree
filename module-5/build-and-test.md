
# The Prototype and Test Phase in Design Thinking

---
### What Does It Mean to Prototype?
---


In the world of design, a prototype is not the final answer. It is the honest question made visible.

When designers build a prototype, they are not saying *"this is it."* They are saying *"this is our best current thinking — now let us find out where it breaks."* A good prototype is built quickly, shared openly, and revised without ego. The goal is not to be right on the first try. The goal is to learn faster than you would by thinking alone.

Consider how this applies to your life.

Your degree plan is a prototype. Your career vision is a prototype. Your 1/3/5-Year Career Plan is a prototype. None of these are final. None of them are supposed to be. What matters is that they are specific enough to test, honest enough to challenge, and flexible enough to revise.

In Design Thinking, the Prototype Phase has two essential characteristics:

**1. Prototypes are tangible.**
An idea in your head cannot be tested. A written roadmap, a timeline, a decision matrix — these can be examined, questioned, and improved. This module asks you to make your plans concrete so you can actually work with them.

**2. Prototypes are meant to fail productively.**
The best thing a prototype can do is reveal a problem while there is still time to fix it. Finding out that your plan has a gap in your freshman year is infinitely better than finding out in your senior year. This module teaches you to stress-test deliberately — to look for the weaknesses in your plan before circumstances force you to find them.

---
### What Does It Mean to Test?
---

Testing, in Design Thinking, is the act of putting your prototype in front of reality and asking: *Does this hold up?*

In professional design, testing involves real users giving real feedback. In the context of your career and degree planning, testing involves a different kind of scrutiny — you are asking your plan to hold up against:

- **The reality of your degree requirements** — Does your academic roadmap actually work within the constraints of your major, your credit requirements, and your timeline?
- **The reality of your career field** — Does the career you are planning for actually align with what the job market demands?
- **The reality of uncertainty** — What happens when something unexpected changes? Are you flexible enough to adapt without starting over?
- **The reality of your own growth** — You are not the same person you will be in two years. Does your plan have room to evolve with you?

Testing is not about pessimism. It is about preparation. The designers who test their prototypes rigorously are not the ones who expect failure — they are the ones who are so committed to success that they refuse to leave anything to chance.

---
### Why Freshmen Need This Phase Most
---

Here is a difficult truth: most first-year students build plans that have never been tested.

They choose a major based on a general interest. They picture a career based on a vague image. They assume that if they follow the expected path, everything will fall into place. This is not a character flaw — it is simply the result of having limited experience with how complex and unpredictable academic and professional paths actually are.

The Prototype and Test Phase is the antidote to that fragility.

By deliberately stress-testing your plan now — asking *"what if this does not work?"* before you need the answer — you build something far more valuable than certainty. You build **resilience**. You develop the habit of treating your life plan as something you actively design and refine, rather than something that simply happens to you.

Research on career development consistently shows that students who engage in this kind of deliberate, scenario-based planning are more adaptable, more confident, and more satisfied with their outcomes — not because everything went according to plan, but because they were prepared to adjust when it did not.


# Building Your Integrated Career-Degree Roadmap

---
### What Is an Integrated Career-Degree Roadmap?
---


In Module 3, you built a Draft Degree Plan — a four-year academic roadmap organized by semester. In Module 4, you built a 1/3/5-Year Career Plan — a timeline of professional milestones across academic, professional, skills, and personal growth dimensions.

These two documents were built separately. Now it is time to merge them.

An **Integrated Career-Degree Roadmap** is a single, unified timeline that places your academic milestones and your career milestones side by side — so you can see, at a glance, how your education and your professional development reinforce each other at every stage.

This integration matters for a fundamental reason: **your degree and your career are not sequential — they are parallel.** Many students think of college as the preparation phase and career as what happens after. In reality, the most successful professionals begin building their careers while they are still in school — through internships, research projects, student organizations, networking, and skill development. The Integrated Roadmap makes that parallel development visible and intentional.

---
### The Four Lanes of Your Roadmap
---


Think of your Integrated Career-Degree Roadmap as a four-lane highway, with each lane representing a different dimension of your development:

**Lane 1 — Academic**
Your courses, major requirements, electives, minors, and certifications. This is your formal degree progression.

**Lane 2 — Professional Experience**
Internships, part-time jobs, research positions, clinical hours, co-ops, and any other hands-on professional experience you plan to pursue.

**Lane 3 — Skills & Competencies**
Technical skills, soft skills, tools, languages, certifications, and workshops that you will develop outside of your formal coursework.

**Lane 4 — Network & Visibility**
Relationships, mentors, professional organizations, conferences, LinkedIn presence, and any platform-building activities that increase your professional visibility.

A strong Integrated Roadmap has activity in all four lanes at all four stages of your college career — not just in the final year when you begin applying for jobs.

---
### The Four Stages of Your Roadmap
---

Your four-year college career can be organized into four developmental stages, each with a distinct focus:

| Stage | Year | Primary Focus |
|-------|------|---------------|
| **Explore** | Year 1 (Freshman) | Orientation, self-discovery, foundational courses, early exposure |
| **Build** | Year 2 (Sophomore) | Deepening knowledge, first professional experiences, skill development |
| **Apply** | Year 3 (Junior) | Substantial internships, leadership roles, specialization, networking |
| **Launch** | Year 4 (Senior) | Capstone projects, full-time job search or graduate school applications, professional identity |

Understanding which stage you are in — and what that stage demands — helps you prioritize your energy and make intentional choices at every point in your academic career.


# Stress-Testing Your Plan

---
### Why You Must Break Your Plan Before Life Does
---

Every plan looks good on paper. The test of a real plan is not how well it works under ideal conditions — it is how well it holds up when conditions change.

Stress-testing is a concept borrowed from engineering and finance. Engineers stress-test bridges by simulating loads far beyond what the bridge will normally carry — not because they expect those loads, but because they need to know where the bridge will fail before anyone's life depends on it. Financial analysts stress-test portfolios by modeling worst-case market scenarios — not because they predict a crash, but because they need to know how much risk the portfolio can absorb.

You need to stress-test your career-degree plan for the same reason.

The questions you are about to ask are not meant to discourage you. They are meant to make your plan stronger. A plan that has been honestly examined and deliberately revised is far more resilient than one that has never been challenged.

---
### The Five Stress-Test Categories
---

There are five categories of pressure that most commonly disrupt academic and career plans. A thorough stress-test examines all five:

**1. Interest Drift**
Your interests will change. Research consistently shows that a significant percentage of college students change their major at least once — often because they discover new interests or find that an interest they expected to love does not translate well to a career.
*Stress-test question: What happens to your plan if your interests shift significantly in Year 2 or Year 3?*

**2. Market Disruption**
Industries evolve. Jobs that exist today may be automated, restructured, or eliminated. New fields emerge that did not exist when you started college.
*Stress-test question: What happens to your plan if the career you are targeting is significantly disrupted by technology or economic change?*

**3. Academic Obstacles**
Courses are harder than expected. A required course is only offered once a year and you missed the enrollment window. A professor withdraws. Your GPA in a prerequisite course does not qualify you for the next level.
*Stress-test question: What happens to your timeline if you need to retake a course, change a sequence, or add a semester?*

**4. Life Interruptions**
Family obligations, financial pressures, health challenges, and personal circumstances do not pause for degree plans.
*Stress-test question: What happens to your plan if you need to reduce your course load, take a leave of absence, or transfer to a different institution?*

**5. Competitive Reality**
The career you want may be more competitive than you realize. The internship you planned for may receive hundreds of applicants. The graduate program you are targeting may have a 10% acceptance rate.
*Stress-test question: What happens to your plan if you do not get the internship, program, or job you were counting on?*


# Contingency Planning — Plan A, Plan B, and Plan C

---
### The Logic of Having More Than One Plan
---


Most people have a Plan A. Very few have a Plan B. Almost no one has a Plan C.

This is a design error.

Experienced professionals and strategic thinkers do not treat their career path as a single, fragile thread. They treat it as a network — multiple possible routes to meaningful, fulfilling work. Having a Plan B is not pessimism. It is not a sign that you lack commitment to Plan A. It is a sign that you understand how the world actually works — and that you have designed your professional life with enough flexibility to navigate it well.

In Design Thinking, this is sometimes called **designing for optionality**: building your skills, relationships, and experiences in ways that open multiple doors rather than betting everything on one.

The framework you will use in this section is straightforward:

- **Plan A** — Your primary path. The career and degree trajectory you are most committed to and most excited about. This is what you pursue with full intention.
- **Plan B** — Your pivot path. A closely related alternative that draws on the same foundation of skills, values, and interests, but points toward a different role, industry, or application.
- **Plan C** — Your resilience path. A broader alternative that may look quite different from Plans A and B, but that you could pursue with integrity and satisfaction if circumstances required a more significant change of direction.

---
### What a Good Contingency Plan Is Not
---


Before you build your contingency plans, it is important to be clear about what they are not.

A contingency plan is **not a backup you do not care about.** If you build a Plan B that holds no genuine interest for you, it will not serve you when you need it. Each plan should be one you could actually commit to — not just a fallback you would tolerate.

A contingency plan is **not a completely different life.** Plans A, B, and C should share a common core — your values, your fundamental strengths, your RIASEC profile, your Ikigai. What changes is the specific application, not who you are.

A contingency plan is **not a sign of failure.** The most successful professionals you will ever meet have changed direction multiple times. What distinguishes them is not that they never pivoted — it is that they pivoted with intention, drawing on a foundation they had deliberately built.


# The Adaptive Professional — Staying Relevant

---
### The Half-Life of Skills
---

Here is a fact that should inform everything you do in college: **the skills that will matter most in your career ten years from now do not all exist yet.**

This is not speculation. The World Economic Forum estimates that more than 40% of core job skills are expected to change significantly within the next five years. Entire job categories are being reshaped by artificial intelligence, automation, and global economic shifts. Fields that seemed stable a decade ago have been transformed beyond recognition.

This does not mean your education is irrelevant. It means your education needs to give you more than just knowledge — it needs to give you the capacity to keep learning, adapting, and growing long after you leave the classroom.

This is the essence of the **Adaptive Professional**: someone who has mastered not just a set of skills, but a set of practices — habits of learning, curiosity, and deliberate development that allow them to remain relevant and effective no matter how their field evolves.

---
### The Four Practices of the Adaptive Professional
---


Research on professional longevity and career resilience points consistently to four practices that distinguish professionals who thrive through change from those who struggle with it:

**Practice 1 — Continuous Learning**
Adaptive professionals treat learning as a permanent activity, not a phase of life that ends with graduation. They read widely, take courses, attend conferences, seek mentors, and deliberately expose themselves to ideas outside their immediate field. They build learning into their weekly routine, not just their free time.

**Practice 2 — Strategic Networking**
Adaptive professionals understand that opportunities, information, and support come through relationships. They invest in building genuine professional networks — not transactional collections of contacts, but real relationships built on mutual respect and shared interest. They give as much as they receive, and they cultivate their networks before they need them.

**Practice 3 — Personal Brand and Visibility**
Adaptive professionals make their expertise visible. They build portfolios, contribute to professional communities, write, present, publish, and engage in conversations that put their thinking on display. They understand that in a competitive professional world, being excellent is necessary but not sufficient — you also need to be known.

**Practice 4 — Reflective Practice**
Adaptive professionals regularly step back to evaluate where they are, where they are going, and whether their current path is still aligned with their values and goals. They do not simply accumulate experience — they extract lessons from it. They use structured reflection to turn what happens to them into wisdom they can apply going forward.


# The Ideate Phase in Design Thinking

---
### What Does It Mean to Ideate?
---


If you have ever been in a brainstorming session where someone said *"there are no bad ideas"* — that is ideation. But ideation in Design Thinking is more disciplined than a free-for-all. It is structured divergent thinking: the deliberate practice of generating many possibilities before committing to any of them.

In professional design, ideation typically follows a clear pattern:

1. **Diverge** — Generate as many ideas as possible without judgment.
2. **Explore** — Investigate the most interesting ideas with curiosity and depth.
3. **Converge** — Narrow the possibilities down based on criteria, constraints, and fit.

Most people skip straight to convergence. They hear "career planning" and immediately think: *"What job is practical? What pays well? What will my family approve of?"* Those are convergence questions — and they are important — but asking them too early kills possibility before it can be explored.

The Ideate Phase asks you to diverge first. To ask: *"What careers actually interest me? What would I explore if practicality were not the first filter?"* This does not mean ignoring reality. It means giving your genuine interests a fair hearing before the constraints close in.

In this module, you will explore your career possibilities through structured lenses — a validated assessment tool, research, and reflection — before converging on the plan you will build in Module 5.

---
### Why Career Ideation Matters for Freshmen
---


Research on career development consistently shows that students who engage in structured career exploration early in college — rather than defaulting to the first plausible option — report higher career satisfaction, stronger academic motivation, and better long-term outcomes.

The reason is straightforward: when you understand *why* you are pursuing a particular career path — when it connects to your values, interests, and strengths — you are far more resilient when challenges arise. You persist not because someone told you to, but because the goal feels genuinely meaningful.

The challenge for most freshmen is that career exploration feels overwhelming. There are thousands of possible careers. Industries change. Job titles that did not exist five years ago are now among the fastest-growing fields. It is easy to feel paralyzed.

This module gives you a structured approach to cut through that overwhelm. Rather than trying to evaluate every career at once, you will use your self-knowledge from Module 2 as a filter. You already know your values, your strengths, your interests, and your SWOT. Now you will use those as a lens to focus your exploration on the careers that are most likely to be a genuine fit.

---
### The Ideate Phase and Creative Courage
---

There is one more thing worth saying before we begin.

Career ideation requires a particular kind of courage — the courage to name what you actually want before you know whether it is achievable. Many students suppress their genuine career interests early because they have been told those interests are impractical, unrealistic, or not serious enough. They choose safer paths not because those paths fit them better, but because those paths feel more defensible.

This module asks you to set that instinct aside — at least temporarily.

The Design Thinking principle behind ideation is simple: **you cannot build a great solution if you never let yourself imagine one.** The Prototype Phase (Module 5) is where you stress-test your ideas against reality. But you cannot prototype something you were too afraid to name.

So as you work through this module, practice noticing when you are censoring yourself. When you find yourself thinking *"I can't put that down — it's not realistic"* — write it down anyway. You can evaluate it later. Right now, your job is to explore.


# The RIASEC Assessment — Discovering Your Career Personality

---
### What Is the RIASEC Model?
---


The **RIASEC model** — also known as the **Holland Code** — is one of the most widely used and research-validated frameworks in career psychology. Developed by psychologist John Holland in the 1950s and refined over decades of research, the model proposes that both people and work environments can be described using six broad interest types.

Understanding your Holland Code does not tell you exactly what career to pursue. What it does is give you a powerful, evidence-based lens for understanding the *kinds* of work environments, tasks, and roles that are most likely to energize and satisfy you.

The six RIASEC types are:

| Code | Type | Description |
|------|------|-------------|
| **R** | **Realistic** | Prefers working with tools, machines, plants, or animals. Values practical, hands-on work. Often drawn to engineering, construction, agriculture, or technical trades. |
| **I** | **Investigative** | Prefers working with ideas, data, and analysis. Values intellectual inquiry and problem-solving. Often drawn to science, research, medicine, or technology. |
| **A** | **Artistic** | Prefers creative expression, unstructured work, and original thinking. Values imagination and aesthetics. Often drawn to design, writing, performing arts, or media. |
| **S** | **Social** | Prefers working with people — helping, teaching, or supporting others. Values relationships and community impact. Often drawn to education, healthcare, counseling, or social work. |
| **E** | **Enterprising** | Prefers leading, persuading, and influencing others. Values ambition, competition, and achievement. Often drawn to business, law, politics, or entrepreneurship. |
| **C** | **Conventional** | Prefers organized, structured work with clear procedures and data. Values accuracy, order, and efficiency. Often drawn to accounting, administration, data management, or finance. |

---
### How the Holland Code Works
---

Your Holland Code is typically expressed as a **three-letter combination** of your top three RIASEC types, in order of strength. For example:

- A student with a Holland Code of **SAE** scores highest in Social, then Artistic, then Enterprising.
- A student with a **IRS** code scores highest in Investigative, then Realistic, then Social.

The power of the Holland Code lies in matching your three-letter profile to career environments that share the same characteristics. Research shows that when people work in environments that match their Holland Code, they tend to report higher job satisfaction, stronger performance, and longer career tenure.

That said, the Holland Code is a *starting point*, not a final answer. It is one lens among many. You will use it alongside your values, strengths, and self-knowledge from Module 2 to build a complete picture.

**Important note on taking the assessment:** The RIASEC assessment is most accurate when you answer based on what genuinely interests you — not what you are good at, not what seems impressive, and not what you think is the "right" answer. Answer quickly and instinctively for the best results.

---
### Understanding Your Results
---

Once you have your Holland Code, the next step is interpretation. Here is how to read your results effectively:

**Strong vs. Weak Codes**
Your top three letters are your primary types, but pay attention to the *strength* of each score. A student with nearly equal scores across four types has more flexibility than one whose top type dominates. Neither pattern is better — but understanding yours helps you interpret the results accurately.

**Adjacent Types**
On the RIASEC hexagon (a visual model showing the six types in a circle), types that are adjacent to each other are more compatible and commonly found together. Types that are opposite each other are less frequently paired. For example:
- **S** (Social) and **E** (Enterprising) are adjacent — common combination.
- **R** (Realistic) and **S** (Social) are opposite — less common, but entirely possible.

**When Results Surprise You**
Sometimes your Holland Code reflects interests you had not consciously recognized. That is the point. If your results surprise you, lean into curiosity rather than dismissal — ask yourself what those results might be revealing about you.

**When Results Confirm What You Already Knew**
Equally valuable. Confirmation from a validated tool gives you confidence to pursue directions you may have been second-guessing.


# Career Exploration — Researching Your Possibilities

---
### From Interest to Information
---


Knowing your Holland Code and your values is the beginning of career ideation — not the end. The next step is to move from *interest* to *information* by researching specific careers with the same seriousness a good designer brings to understanding a problem.

This is important because most people's ideas about careers are based on limited, often distorted information. What you see on television, hear from family members, or absorb from cultural stereotypes is rarely an accurate picture of what a career actually involves day-to-day. Effective career exploration requires going to primary sources.

In this section, you will learn to use three of the most powerful free career research tools available:

**1. O*NET OnLine (www.onetonline.org)**
Maintained by the U.S. Department of Labor, O*NET is the most comprehensive career database in the country. For any occupation, O*NET provides:
- Detailed task and activity descriptions (what people actually do)
- Required knowledge, skills, and abilities
- Work context and environment information
- Education and training requirements
- Median wages and employment outlook data
- Related occupations for further exploration

**2. Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Outlook Handbook (www.bls.gov/ooh)**
The OOH provides career-level data on job growth, salary ranges, employment trends, and required qualifications. It is particularly useful for understanding the *trajectory* of a career — whether it is growing, shrinking, or stable.

**3. LinkedIn (www.linkedin.com)**
LinkedIn is not just a job-searching tool — it is a career research tool. Use it to:
- Find people working in careers that interest you and study their career paths
- Look at job postings to understand what employers actually require
- Research companies in industries that align with your interests
- Identify alumni from your university working in fields you are curious about

---
### The T-Shaped Professional — Depth and Breadth
---


As you research careers, keep in mind a concept that will shape how you think about your academic and professional development: the **T-Shaped Professional**.

The T-shape metaphor describes a professional who has:
- **Depth** (the vertical bar of the T) — deep expertise in one specific area
- **Breadth** (the horizontal bar of the T) — working knowledge across multiple adjacent areas

In today's workplace, T-shaped professionals are among the most valuable. A nurse who deeply understands clinical care *and* has working knowledge of data analytics and patient communication is more versatile and employable than one with clinical knowledge alone.

As you research careers, begin thinking about what your T might look like:
- What is the deep expertise you want to develop? (Your vertical bar)
- What adjacent skills and knowledge will make you more effective and resilient? (Your horizontal bar)

This will directly inform your degree plan as you continue to refine it.


# Crafting Your Career Vision Statement

---
### What Is a Career Vision Statement?
---


A **Career Vision Statement** is a clear, compelling, and personally meaningful description of the professional future you are working toward. It is not a job title. It is not a LinkedIn summary. It is not a prediction of exactly what will happen.

A Career Vision Statement answers three questions simultaneously:

1. **What kind of work do I want to do?** — The nature and substance of your professional contribution.
2. **Who do I want to serve or impact?** — The people, communities, or problems your work addresses.
3. **What kind of professional do I want to become?** — The values, qualities, and character you want to embody in your career.

The best Career Vision Statements are:
- **Specific enough** to be meaningful and directional
- **Expansive enough** to allow for growth and evolution
- **Authentic** — grounded in genuine self-knowledge, not aspiration-performance
- **Forward-looking** — describing a future state, not your current situation

---
### Vision vs. Goal — Understanding the Difference
---


Students often confuse Career Vision with Career Goals. They are related but distinct:

| | Career Vision | Career Goal |
|---|---|---|
| **Time horizon** | Long-term (10–20 years out) | Short to medium term (1–5 years) |
| **Nature** | Directional and aspirational | Specific and measurable |
| **Function** | Provides meaning and motivation | Provides structure and accountability |
| **Example** | *"To lead healthcare innovation that makes quality care accessible to underserved communities"* | *"Complete a healthcare administration internship by junior year"* |

Your Career Vision is the *north star*. Your Career Goals (which you will develop in Section 4.5) are the *steps* that move you toward it.

You need both. Vision without goals is a dream. Goals without vision are tasks without meaning.


# Building Your 1/3/5-Year Career Plan

---
### From Vision to Action — The Career Plan
---


A Career Vision Statement tells you *where* you want to go. A Career Plan tells you *how* you will get there.

The **1/3/5-Year Career Plan** is a structured timeline that breaks your career vision into concrete milestones across three time horizons:

- **1-Year Milestones** — What you will accomplish by the end of your first year of college. These should be specific, actionable, and realistic. They are the immediate steps that put you in motion.

- **3-Year Milestones** — Where you want to be by the end of your junior year. These are intermediate goals that build on your first-year steps and prepare you for the final push toward your career vision.

- **5-Year Milestones** — Where you want to be approximately two years after graduation (or five years from now). These are longer-horizon goals that describe the early stage of your career becoming real.

Each time horizon should include milestones across four dimensions:

| Dimension | Description |
|-----------|-------------|
| **Academic** | Coursework, GPA, majors/minors, academic achievements |
| **Professional** | Internships, jobs, volunteer work, shadowing, networking |
| **Skill Development** | Technical skills, soft skills, certifications, languages |
| **Personal Growth** | Self-awareness, leadership, mentorship, community involvement |

---
### Why Freshmen Need a Career Plan — Not Just a Major
---


Many students believe that choosing the right major is equivalent to having a career plan. It is not.

A major is an academic structure. A career is a professional life. The connection between the two is real — but it is not automatic. Students who actively plan their careers alongside their academic programs consistently outperform those who assume their major will handle everything.

The reason is simple: the most important career-building experiences in college — internships, research opportunities, leadership roles, professional networks, relevant skills — do not happen by accident. They happen because students who planned for them made them happen, often starting in their first year.

Your 1/3/5-Year Career Plan is not meant to be a rigid script. Life will change it. New opportunities will emerge. Your interests will evolve. But having a plan means you will adapt *intentionally* — not simply drift.


# Synthesizing Your Ideate Phase

---
### From Exploration to Direction
---


You have done significant work in this module. You set your ideation intentions. You completed the RIASEC Assessment and built a full profile. You researched three careers in depth. You wrote a Career Vision Statement. You built a 1/3/5-Year Career Plan.

Taken together, these activities represent something important: you have moved from *vague career curiosity* to *documented career direction*. That is not a small thing. Most people — including many college graduates — never do this work in a structured, intentional way.

Now it is time to step back and synthesize. What does all of this mean, taken together? What is the most important thing you now know about your career direction that you did not know at the start of this module? And where are the remaining questions — the things you still need to figure out?

Those remaining questions are exactly what Module 5 is designed to address.




# The Empathize Phase in Design Thinking

---
### What Does It Mean to Empathize with Yourself?
---


Most of us go through life without ever truly examining who we are. We form habits, develop preferences, and make decisions — often without understanding *why*. We say we want one thing and consistently do another. We know what we are good at in theory, but struggle to articulate it when it matters.

The Empathize Phase of Design Thinking is designed to close that gap.

In design practice, empathy is the disciplined act of setting aside assumptions and truly understanding a person's lived experience. Designers observe, interview, and immerse themselves in the lives of the people they are designing for — not to project their own ideas onto them, but to genuinely understand what those people need, feel, think, and do.

**Empathizing with yourself requires the same discipline.**

It means setting aside the version of yourself you *wish* you were, the version your parents see, or the version you perform for others — and looking honestly at who you actually are right now. Your real strengths. Your actual weaknesses. Your genuine interests, not the ones that sound impressive. Your true fears, not just the ones you are comfortable admitting.

This kind of self-knowledge is not easy. But it is the only honest foundation for designing a life that actually fits you.

---
### Why Self-Knowledge Is the Foundation of Good Decision-Making
---

Consider two students entering college:

**Student A** chooses a major because their parents suggested it, because it sounds stable, and because they are not sure what else to do. They have never seriously examined their own strengths or interests. Three semesters in, they are struggling in courses they do not enjoy, questioning whether college was the right choice, and feeling lost.

**Student B** spends time in their first semester doing exactly what you are doing now — systematically examining who they are, what they value, and what genuinely energizes them. They discover unexpected strengths. They identify patterns in their interests they had never noticed. They make an informed, intentional choice about their direction. Three semesters in, they are still adjusting and refining — but they are adjusting *thoughtfully*, not drifting.

The difference is not intelligence. It is not even effort. It is **self-knowledge**.

Research in career development consistently shows that students who engage in structured self-reflection early in their academic careers are more likely to:
- Choose majors that align with their strengths and interests
- Persist through academic challenges with greater resilience
- Report higher satisfaction with both their academic and professional lives
- Make faster progress toward meaningful career goals

Self-knowledge is not a luxury. It is a practical, strategic advantage.

---
### The Role of Curiosity: Asking Better Questions About Yourself
---

The Empathize Phase is driven by **curiosity** — not judgment.

One of the most common mistakes students make when beginning a self-assessment is approaching it with a fixed idea of what the answers should be. They answer questions in ways that sound good rather than ways that are honest. They skip over uncomfortable insights. They interpret their results through the lens of what they think they *should* want.

This module asks you to resist that impulse.

Think of yourself as a researcher studying a fascinating subject — and that subject happens to be you. A good researcher does not decide what they want to find before they look. They observe carefully, follow unexpected threads, sit with surprising data, and let the evidence lead them to conclusions.

As you work through this module, practice asking:
- *Why do I believe that about myself?*
- *Is that actually true — or is it something I was told?*
- *What does this result reveal that I have not considered before?*
- *What would I discover if I looked more honestly?*

The goal is not a flattering self-portrait. The goal is an **accurate** one — because accurate self-knowledge is what makes good decisions possible.

---

---

This module uses a carefully selected set of self-assessment tools, each designed to illuminate a different dimension of who you are. No single tool tells the whole story — but together, they produce a rich, multi-dimensional picture.

# Exploring Your Interests and Values

---
### What Are Values and Why Do They Guide Your Choices?
---


**Values** are the principles and beliefs that are most important to you — the things you are not willing to compromise when making significant decisions. They are not the same as goals (what you want to achieve) or preferences (what you enjoy). Values are deeper than both. They are the *criteria* by which you evaluate whether a path is worth taking.

Consider some examples:

- A student who values **independence** may feel stifled in a highly structured work environment — regardless of the salary.
- A student who values **creativity** may struggle in a role defined by repetitive, standardized tasks — even if the role is prestigious.
- A student who values **community** may find great fulfillment in a public service career that pays modestly — and considerable emptiness in a high-paying role that isolates them.

Understanding your values is not about romanticizing your future. It is about making **informed trade-offs**. Every career choice involves trade-offs. Knowing your values helps you trade wisely.

---
### 2.2.2 Common Core Values in Academic and Career Decision-Making
---

While every person's value system is unique, research in career development has identified a set of values that frequently influence academic and career decisions. Familiarizing yourself with these categories can help you identify and name your own.

**Achievement & Mastery**
Doing challenging work, developing expertise, being recognized for competence.

**Autonomy & Independence**
Making your own decisions, working without close supervision, charting your own course.

**Creativity & Innovation**
Generating new ideas, solving problems in original ways, expressing yourself.

**Security & Stability**
Predictable income, clear career paths, job security, low financial risk.

**Connection & Community**
Building relationships, belonging to something larger than yourself, serving others.

**Impact & Purpose**
Making a meaningful difference, contributing to causes larger than personal gain.

**Wealth & Advancement**
Financial success, upward mobility, status, professional recognition.

**Learning & Intellectual Challenge**
Continuous growth, exposure to new ideas, working in intellectually stimulating environments.

**Balance & Wellness**
Protecting time for family, health, hobbies, and rest alongside professional ambitions.

None of these values is better than any other. What matters is that you identify *yours* honestly — not what you think sounds most admirable.

---
### Interests vs. Passions vs. Skills: Understanding the Difference
---

These three terms are frequently used interchangeably — and that confusion leads to poor decisions. Let us distinguish them precisely.

**Interests** are the subjects, activities, or environments that naturally draw your attention. You find yourself reading about them without being assigned to, gravitating toward them in conversation, noticing them in the world around you. Interests do not require talent — you can be interested in something you are not yet good at.

**Passions** are interests with emotional intensity. A passion is something you feel strongly about — something that energizes rather than drains you, that you return to even when it is difficult, that connects to your sense of meaning or identity. Not everyone has a single consuming passion, and that is perfectly normal. But most people have areas of genuine enthusiasm that, when identified, point toward meaningful directions.

**Skills** are things you can do well — developed through practice, education, or experience. Skills are learnable. You can build skills in areas that interest you. However, skills alone, disconnected from interest or meaning, rarely produce long-term career satisfaction.

The most sustainable career paths tend to live at the intersection of all three: **what you are genuinely interested in, what you care about deeply, and what you are developing the ability to do well.**

Understanding the difference helps you avoid two common traps:
1. Choosing a career based only on what you are currently good at, ignoring what you actually care about.
2. Pursuing a passion without honestly assessing whether you are building the skills to make it viable.


# SWOT Analysis — Knowing Your Personal Landscape

---
### What Is a SWOT Analysis?
---


**SWOT Analysis** is one of the most widely used strategic planning tools in the world. Originally developed for business and organizational contexts, it has been powerfully adapted for personal and career development. SWOT is an acronym that stands for:

| Letter | Dimension | What It Examines |
|--------|-----------|-----------------|
| **S** | Strengths | Internal capabilities and advantages you currently possess |
| **W** | Weaknesses | Internal limitations, gaps, or areas for development |
| **O** | Opportunities | External conditions, resources, or circumstances you can leverage |
| **T** | Threats | External challenges, risks, or obstacles that may work against you |

The power of SWOT lies in its structure. By examining yourself across all four dimensions simultaneously, you gain a complete picture of your current situation — not just a catalog of your best qualities, and not just a list of your concerns, but an honest, integrated view of where you stand.

---
### SWOT in Business vs. SWOT for Personal Development
---

In a business context, a SWOT analysis evaluates an organization's competitive position in a market. A company might identify its strong brand reputation as a Strength, its limited distribution network as a Weakness, an emerging market as an Opportunity, and a new competitor as a Threat.

For personal development, the logic is exactly the same — but the focus shifts entirely to **you**:

- **Strengths** are the internal qualities, skills, and characteristics that give you an advantage — things you do well, naturally or through effort.
- **Weaknesses** are the internal areas where you currently struggle, lack experience, or need development. Importantly, weaknesses in a SWOT analysis are not character flaws — they are honest gaps in your current capabilities.
- **Opportunities** are external factors you can take advantage of — available resources, institutional support, timing, relationships, and circumstances in your environment.
- **Threats** are external pressures that may make your path more difficult — financial constraints, competitive job markets, limited access to certain experiences, or systemic barriers.

Notice that Strengths and Weaknesses are **internal** — they are within your control and can change through effort. Opportunities and Threats are **external** — they exist in the environment around you and require strategic awareness to navigate.

---
### Identifying Your Strengths
---


A strength, in the context of personal SWOT analysis, is any quality, skill, ability, or characteristic that consistently enables you to perform well, solve problems, or add value in some domain of your life.

Strengths can be:
- **Technical** — specific skills or knowledge areas (e.g., strong mathematical reasoning, proficiency in a language, coding experience)
- **Interpersonal** — how you relate to and work with others (e.g., active listening, conflict resolution, the ability to motivate people)
- **Cognitive** — how you think and process information (e.g., analytical thinking, pattern recognition, creative problem-solving)
- **Character-Based** — qualities that define how you operate (e.g., persistence, integrity, curiosity, adaptability)

One useful test: **Can you give a specific, concrete example of this strength in action?** If you cannot, it may be an aspiration rather than an actual current strength. Both have value — but they belong in different places in your analysis.

---
### Acknowledging Your Weaknesses
---


This is the section most people rush through or do dishonestly.

A weakness, in this framework, is not a moral failing or a permanent deficiency. It is simply an area where your current capabilities, habits, or tendencies create friction — where you consistently underperform, avoid tasks, or struggle in ways that limit your effectiveness.

Common academic and career-relevant weaknesses students identify include:
- Difficulty managing time or staying organized without external structure
- Tendency to procrastinate, especially on large or ambiguous tasks
- Limited experience in professional settings or specific skill areas
- Discomfort with public speaking, writing, or analytical reasoning
- Difficulty asking for help or advocating for oneself
- Impulsive decision-making under pressure

> 🔎 **Important:** The purpose of naming a weakness is not to dwell on it — it is to make it *visible* so you can address it strategically. A weakness you cannot see cannot be managed. A weakness you acknowledge can be planned around, worked on, or compensated for.

---
### Recognizing Opportunities
---

Opportunities are the external resources, conditions, and circumstances available to you that — if you take advantage of them — could meaningfully accelerate your academic or professional development.

As a college student, your opportunities include:

- **Institutional Resources:** Academic advising, career services, tutoring centers, writing labs, research opportunities, study abroad programs, internship pipelines
- **Network Access:** Faculty, alumni, professionals who visit campus, peer mentors, professional associations
- **Timing:** The fact that you are early in your career is itself an opportunity — you have time to explore, make mistakes, and course-correct before the stakes are high
- **Technology:** Online learning platforms, professional networking tools, remote internship opportunities, AI-assisted research tools
- **Your Major and CECS:** The flexibility of an interdisciplinary college means you have more latitude to design a non-traditional path than students in more rigid programs

The key question for Opportunities is not *"what opportunities exist in general?"* but *"what opportunities exist for me, right now, in my specific situation?"*

---
### Understanding Threats
---

Threats are external factors that could make your path more difficult — conditions in your environment that you did not create and cannot entirely control, but which you must account for in your planning.

Common threats students encounter include:

- **Financial pressure** that limits the time available to invest in unpaid internships, extracurriculars, or extended degree timelines
- **A competitive job market** in fields of high interest
- **Limited access** to professional networks, mentors, or geographic markets
- **Rapidly changing industries** where skills can become obsolete faster than degrees are completed
- **Family obligations** that compete with academic focus
- **Mental health challenges** or external stressors that affect academic performance

Naming threats is not pessimism — it is strategic realism. A threat you have identified can be planned around. A threat you have ignored can derail you.


# Additional Self-Assessment Tools

---
### 2.4.1 The Wheel of Life
---

The **Wheel of Life** is a visual self-assessment tool used in coaching and personal development to evaluate how balanced and satisfied you currently feel across the key dimensions of your life. It is based on the idea that a fulfilling life is not built on success in a single area — it requires reasonable attention to multiple domains simultaneously.

The standard Wheel of Life includes eight dimensions:

| Dimension | What It Encompasses |
|-----------|-------------------|
| **Academic / Career** | Your engagement with school, work, and professional development |
| **Financial** | Your relationship with money, budgeting, and financial stability |
| **Health & Wellness** | Physical health, exercise, sleep, nutrition |
| **Mental & Emotional Health** | Stress levels, emotional regulation, mental wellbeing |
| **Relationships** | Quality of family, friendships, and social connections |
| **Personal Growth** | Learning, self-improvement, spiritual or philosophical development |
| **Fun & Recreation** | Hobbies, leisure, creativity, play |
| **Physical Environment** | Your living situation, sense of safety, and surrounding conditions |

The value of the Wheel of Life for a first-year student is significant: it often reveals that dissatisfaction in one area (say, academic performance) is being driven by neglect in another (such as mental health or sleep). You cannot design a sustainable academic and career plan without acknowledging the full context of your life.

---
### The Ikigai Framework
---

**Ikigai** is a Japanese concept that roughly translates to "reason for being" — the sense that your life has purpose, direction, and meaning. In career development, the Ikigai framework is represented as four overlapping circles:

```
        What you LOVE
              ↓
    [Passion]   [Mission]
         ↓           ↓
What you're    What the
GOOD AT   ←→  world NEEDS
         ↑           ↑
    [Vocation]  [Profession]
              ↑
        What you can be
           PAID FOR
```

| Intersection | What It Represents |
|--------------|-------------------|
| **Passion** | What you love AND are good at |
| **Mission** | What you love AND what the world needs |
| **Vocation** | What you are good at AND can be paid for |
| **Profession** | What the world needs AND you can be paid for |
| **Ikigai** | The center — where all four circles overlap |

The Ikigai framework does not demand that every student immediately identify their perfect career intersection. That is unrealistic, and it is not the point. The point is to begin thinking about your decisions across multiple dimensions simultaneously — not just *"what pays well?"* but *"what do I care about, what am I developing skill in, and what does the world actually need?"*

---
### Clifton Strengths
---


Two of the most research-validated strengths assessments available are **CliftonStrengths** (developed by Gallup) and **VIA Character Strengths** (developed by the VIA Institute on Character). Both are grounded in positive psychology — the scientific study of what makes people thrive.

**CliftonStrengths** identifies 34 talent themes (such as Achiever, Learner, Strategic, Empathy, Command) and ranks them based on where your natural patterns of thinking, feeling, and behaving are strongest. Your top themes are called your **Signature Strengths**.

**VIA Character Strengths** identifies 24 character strengths (such as Creativity, Curiosity, Bravery, Kindness, Perseverance) that represent your core positive traits. Your top strengths are called your **Signature Character Strengths**.

Both assessments share a common premise: **you are more effective, engaged, and fulfilled when you work from your strengths** rather than spending all your energy compensating for weaknesses. This does not mean weaknesses should be ignored — it means that building a life primarily around your natural strengths is a more sustainable strategy than trying to be someone you are not.

> 📌 **Access:** The VIA Character Strengths survey is available for free at [viacharacter.org](https://www.viacharacter.org). Your institution may provide free access to CliftonStrengths — check with your academic advisor or CECS office.


# Empathy Mapping — Understanding Your Own Story

---
### What Is an Empathy Map?
---


An **Empathy Map** is a visual tool developed in the design thinking community to help designers deeply understand the people they are designing for. It organizes observations about a person into four categories:

| Quadrant | Question |
|----------|----------|
| **THINK** | What does this person think and believe about their situation? |
| **FEEL** | What emotions drive this person's behavior? |
| **SAY** | What does this person say — out loud, to others — about their situation? |
| **DO** | What does this person actually do — their behaviors and actions? |

The most powerful insight produced by an Empathy Map often comes from the **gaps between quadrants** — for example, when what a person *says* does not match what they *do*, or when what they *feel* contradicts what they *think*. These gaps reveal the tensions, fears, and contradictions that shape behavior.

---
### Adapting the Empathy Map to Self-Discovery
---

When you apply the Empathy Map to yourself, something interesting happens: you are forced to observe yourself from the outside — as a researcher would observe a subject — rather than simply narrating your internal experience from the inside.

This shift in perspective is genuinely difficult. Most of us have strong narratives about ourselves that we have been telling for years. The Empathy Map asks you to challenge those narratives with honest observation.

For your **Personal Empathy Map**, each quadrant has a specific focus:

**THINK — Your Beliefs and Inner Narrative**
What do you believe about yourself, about college, about your future? What assumptions are you operating from that you have never fully examined? What does the voice in your head tell you when you face challenges?

**FEEL — Your Emotional Landscape**
What emotions are most present in your life right now? What are you excited about? What are you afraid of? What makes you feel engaged, energized, frustrated, or numb? Emotions are data — do not dismiss them.

**SAY — Your Public Voice**
What do you tell people about your goals and plans? What story do you present to parents, friends, advisors? How does your public voice differ — if it does — from your private thoughts?

**DO — Your Actual Behaviors**
What do your habits and daily choices reveal about what you actually value and prioritize? How do you spend your time? What do you consistently follow through on — and what do you consistently avoid?


# Synthesizing What You've Learned About Yourself

---
### Patterns and Themes: What Do Your Results Have in Common?
---

By now, you have completed five distinct self-assessment activities — Intention Setting, Values and Interests Inventory, Personal SWOT Analysis, Multi-Tool Assessment, and Personal Empathy Map. Each one illuminated a different facet of who you are.

The synthesis step is where the real insight happens.

Think of each assessment as a different light source shone on the same object. Individually, each light reveals something the others cannot. But when you step back and look at the object from all angles simultaneously — when you look for what is consistently visible across all five perspectives — you see it clearly for the first time.

As you review all five assessments together, look for:

**Consistent themes** — qualities, interests, or patterns that appear across multiple assessments. If the same value shows up in your Values Inventory, your Ikigai Map, your SWOT Strengths, and your Empathy Map, that is not coincidence — it is signal.

**Productive tensions** — places where two assessments seem to contradict each other. These contradictions often point to important growth edges or unresolved questions that deserve further exploration.

**Emerging directions** — even at this early stage, patterns in your self-assessment data often point toward general directions — types of work, types of environments, types of problems — that are worth exploring further in Module 3.

---
### From Self-Data to Self-Insight: Making Meaning of Your Assessments
---


Data alone is not insight. A long list of your values, strengths, and interests is not the same as understanding yourself. Insight requires interpretation — the disciplined act of looking at your data and asking: *What does this mean? What does this suggest? What does this ask of me?*

As you write your final synthesis reflection, practice moving from description to interpretation:

| Description (data) | Interpretation (insight) |
|--------------------|--------------------------|
| *"My top values are Creativity and Independence."* | *"This suggests I will thrive in roles that offer autonomy and the freedom to generate original work — and may struggle in highly structured, compliance-oriented environments."* |
| *"My Empathy Map shows a gap between SAY and DO — I say I value health, but I rarely exercise or sleep consistently."* | *"This tension suggests that academic stress is overriding my stated priorities. I need to build health habits into my schedule before they become a performance liability."* |
| *"My SWOT Opportunities section is mostly blank."* | *"I have not yet learned what resources are available to me. Before I can leverage opportunities, I need to spend time actively exploring what this institution offers."* |

The goal of your synthesis reflection is to produce **5–7 genuine, interpreted insights** — statements that are true, specific, non-obvious, and actionable.

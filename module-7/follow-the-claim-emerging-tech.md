# Follow the Claim

## Purpose

Emerging technology is one of the richest domains for critical thinking practice: claims move fast, are often poorly understood by the public, and frequently come from sources with a direct financial or reputational incentive to overstate them. This assignment trains you to trace a claim to its source, test the evidence behind it, argue against your own position honestly, and recognize your own bias before it recognizes you.

---

## Instructions

### Step 1: Choose Your Claim

Find a claim currently circulating about an emerging technology — AI, quantum computing, biotech/CRISPR, autonomous vehicles, brain-computer interfaces, crypto/blockchain, robotics, or a comparable field. The claim should be one of the following:

- A **capability claim** ("this system can now do X")
- A **risk claim** ("this technology poses danger Y")
- An **inevitability claim** ("this will change everything by [year]")

**You must find your own current claim.** Do not use the examples below — they exist only to show the *type* of claim to look for:

- "AI model X can now reason like a PhD student"
- "This app can detect disease Y from a selfie with 99% accuracy"
- "Self-driving cars are safer than human drivers"
- "Quantum computer breaks encryption in record time"

---

### Step 2: Complete the Four-Part Analysis

#### Part 1 — Trace It (Source Archaeology)

Follow the claim backward through every stage it passed through:

> viral tweet/headline → press release → actual paper, patent, or technical report → fine print

Identify exactly where hedging language disappeared. Phrases like *"in preliminary testing"* or *"under controlled conditions"* are often present in the original source and quietly dropped by the time the claim reaches a headline. Document each hop and note what changed.

#### Part 2 — Stress-Test It (Evidence Audit)

Answer each of the following with specifics, not generalities:

- What is the actual benchmark, sample size, or test condition behind the claim?
- Was the claim evaluated by an independent third party, or only by the people selling/promoting it?
- Does "state of the art" mean *better than everything that exists*, or *better than the one comparison point the source chose to report*?
- Does the source have a financial or reputational stake (funding round, stock price, hype cycle, upcoming product launch) that could have shaped how the claim was framed?

#### Part 3 — Steelman the Opposite

Write the **strongest possible expert skeptic's case** against the claim. This is not generic "tech is bad" cynicism — it must be a specific, technical objection a knowledgeable critic would actually raise. Examples of the *shape* of a good steelman:

- "Accuracy on a curated test set doesn't generalize to real-world conditions."
- "The underlying risk is real, but the claimed timeline is not supported by current evidence."
- "This solves a narrow, specific case that is being presented as a general solution."

#### Part 4 — Locate Your Own Bias

Emerging technology is unusually effective at triggering pre-existing bias — people tend to arrive as either techno-optimists or techno-skeptics *before* they see any evidence. Answer honestly:

- Which camp did you lean toward before you started researching?
- How did that lean shape which sources you trusted more readily, and which you scrutinized harder?

This section is graded on **honesty and self-awareness**, not on reaching a "correct" conclusion.

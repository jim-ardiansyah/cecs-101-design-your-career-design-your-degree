# 48-Hour Tool Adoption Challenge
### Building Adaptability in the Age of AI

Technology doesn't wait for you to feel ready. New tools — especially AI-powered ones — show up constantly, and the people who thrive are the ones who can learn them quickly instead of avoiding them. This assignment puts you in that exact situation on purpose: you'll pick an unfamiliar tool, give yourself only 48 hours to get functional with it, and pay close attention to *how* you learn, not just *what* you learn.

---

## Learning Objective

By the end of this assignment, you will be able to:
- Learn a new, unfamiliar tool under a real time constraint
- Identify the specific strategies that help you adapt quickly
- Reflect honestly on the discomfort of not knowing something yet, and how you moved through it

---

## Step 1 — Choose Your Tool (Do this first, before the clock starts)

Select a tool or app you have **never used before**. It should be:

- **New to you** — not something you've dabbled in before
- **Ideally AI-related** (an AI writing tool, image generator, AI-powered productivity app, coding assistant, transcription tool, etc.) — though a non-AI tool is acceptable if you check with your instructor first
- **Free or accessible** without a paid subscription
- **Realistic in scope** — something you could plausibly learn the basics of in two days, not a professional-grade software suite that takes months to master

**Need ideas?** Ask your instructor for a list of suggested tools, or propose your own for approval before starting the clock.

## Step 3 — Learn the Tool (Over the Next 48 Hours)

Dive in. Explore the tool, watch tutorials if needed, experiment, make mistakes, and figure it out. There's no single "right" way to do this — the point is to notice your *own* process as you go.

### Journal Requirement

Keep a short journal during the 48 hours. You don't need to write an essay each time — a few honest sentences per entry is enough. Make **at least 3 journal entries** across the two days (for example: one shortly after you start, one partway through, and one near the end).

For each entry, note:
- **What you tried** (what did you do in this session?)
- **What was confusing or frustrating** (where did you get stuck? What made you want to give up?)
- **What helped** (a tutorial, a friend, trial and error, reading the FAQ, something else?)
- **How you felt** (be honest — overwhelmed, curious, bored, excited, embarrassed, etc.)

**Journal Entry 1** — *(shortly after starting)*
What I tried:
I started by exploring Speechify and learning how to upload a PDF. I used one of my English 355 readings because I wanted to test the tool on something I would actually have to read for class. I tested the basic text-to-speech feature, changed the reading speed, tried different voices, and followed along with the highlighted text.
What was confusing or frustrating:
Uploading the PDF itself was easy, but I quickly realized that getting Speechify to read an academic PDF correctly could be more complicated. Some of the formatting in the document did not transfer perfectly into the text-to-speech. Things like headings, paragraph breaks, citations, and other parts of the PDF could make the reading sound less natural. I also noticed that some words or names could be pronounced incorrectly. This was frustrating because if Speechify reads something incorrectly, I still need to recognize the mistake instead of blindly trusting the audio.
What helped:
I slowed the reading down and followed along with the actual PDF instead of only listening. I also experimented with different voices and speeds to see which combination was easiest for me to understand. I realized that I needed to treat Speechify as a tool to help me read rather than something that could completely do the reading for me.
How I felt:
At first, I thought the challenge would be extremely easy because the basic idea of Speechify is straightforward. After actually testing it with an academic PDF, I realized there were more things I needed to figure out than I expected. I became more curious about whether I could actually make the tool useful for my English 355 workload.

**Journal Entry 2** — *(partway through)*
What I tried:
I spent more time testing Speechify with a longer section of my English 355 reading. Instead of only listening to a few paragraphs, I tried using it for a larger portion of the assignment. I experimented with different speeds and tested whether I could understand the material while doing something simple like taking notes or following along on my computer. I also paid attention to how Speechify handled citations, headings, unfamiliar words, and sections with unusual formatting.
What was confusing or frustrating:
One problem I noticed was that listening faster does not necessarily mean I am learning faster. When I increased the speed too much, I could get through the pages more quickly, but I started missing details and had to go back. I also had trouble when the PDF contained information that was not meant to be read like normal paragraphs. Citations, page elements, and formatting could interrupt the flow of the audio. Some academic words and names also sounded strange when pronounced by the AI voice.
What helped:
Trial and error helped me the most. I started changing one setting at a time instead of randomly changing everything. I found that following along visually while listening helped me catch mistakes and stay focused. I also started breaking the reading into smaller sections instead of trying to listen to the entire PDF in one sitting.
How I felt:
I was starting to feel more comfortable, but I was also realizing that using AI does not automatically make schoolwork effortless. I actually had to learn how to use the tool correctly for my specific situation. That made the challenge feel more meaningful because I was solving an actual problem instead of just learning where different buttons were.

**Journal Entry 3** — *(near the end)*
What I tried:
I tested the workflow I had developed on another long section of my English 355 reading. I used the speed and voice settings that I preferred, followed along with the PDF, paused when I came across something confusing, and went back over sections when I realized I had lost focus. I also experimented with using Speechify while taking notes so I could listen to the reading and record important ideas at the same time.
What was confusing or frustrating:
The biggest challenge was comprehension. It is easy to think that because the words are being read aloud, I am automatically understanding everything. However, academic writing can still be complicated when I hear it instead of reading it. Sometimes I had to pause the audio and reread a paragraph myself because I needed more time to understand what the author was saying. I also learned that Speechify cannot always recognize the intended structure of a complicated PDF perfectly.
What helped:
The biggest thing that helped was creating my own system instead of expecting Speechify to do everything for me. I found that listening at a comfortable speed, following along visually, pausing when necessary, and taking notes worked better than simply pressing play and letting the entire PDF run. I also learned which problems I needed to watch for, such as incorrect pronunciation and awkward formatting.
How I felt:
By this point, I felt much more confident using Speechify. I started the challenge thinking I was simply learning a text-to-speech app, but I ended up learning how to adjust a technology tool to fit the way I personally learn. I also felt like I had found something that could actually make my long English 355 readings feel less intimidating.

## Step 5 — Final Reflection (Write this after your 48 hours are up)

Write a reflection of **8–12 sentences** responding to the following:

- What made this tool difficult to learn at first? Be specific.
- Which strategy helped you adapt the *fastest* — and why do you think it worked?
- Did your feelings about the challenge change from the beginning to the end? How?
- What does this experience tell you about how you personally learn new things under pressure?
- How might this same approach help you the next time a new tool, app, or AI system shows up unexpectedly at school or work?

**My Reflection:**
At first, I thought Speechify would be extremely easy to learn because its main purpose is simply to read text aloud. However, once I used it with an actual English 355 PDF, I realized that academic documents can create problems that are not obvious at first. Speechify does not always handle complicated formatting, citations, headings, or unfamiliar words perfectly, so I had to pay attention to whether the text was actually being read correctly. Another challenge was figuring out the right reading speed because listening faster allowed me to get through more pages, but I sometimes understood less of what I was hearing. The strategy that helped me adapt the fastest was testing the tool with a real assignment and changing one setting at a time instead of trying to learn every feature at once. I also learned that I need to follow along with the PDF and occasionally reread difficult sections rather than assuming that listening automatically means I understand the material. This was useful for me because I don't enjoy reading extremely long PDFs, which can make English 355 assignments feel overwhelming before I even start them. By the end of the challenge, I felt more confident because I had created a system that made long readings feel more manageable instead of simply expecting the technology to solve everything. This experience showed me that I learn new technology best through hands-on experimentation and by connecting it to a problem I actually have. I also learned that adapting to a new tool sometimes means figuring out its limitations just as much as learning its features. If I encounter a new AI tool at school or work, I can use the same approach by starting with a specific goal, experimenting with the tool, identifying problems, and adjusting my approach until I find what works. The challenge taught me that becoming comfortable with new technology is less about immediately knowing how to use it and more about being willing to troubleshoot and adapt when things don't work perfectly.

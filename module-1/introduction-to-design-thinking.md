


# Introduction to Design Thinking


---
### What Is Design Thinking? Origins and Core Principles
---


Design Thinking originated in the world of product and industrial design. It was developed and refined at Stanford University's **d.school (Hasso Plattner Institute of Design)** and popularized by the global design firm **IDEO**. Over time, it moved far beyond design studios and engineering labs. Today, it is used by educators, entrepreneurs, healthcare professionals, policymakers, and organizations around the world to solve complex, human-centered problems.

At its core, Design Thinking is built on a simple but powerful premise:

> **Start with the human. Build from there.**

Rather than beginning with assumptions, rules, or predetermined solutions, Design Thinking starts with deep understanding — of people, their needs, their experiences, and their contexts. From that understanding, everything else follows.

**The five core principles of Design Thinking:**

1. **Human-Centered** — Every decision starts with understanding people, not systems or theories.
2. **Collaborative** — Good design emerges from multiple perspectives. (In this course, your "collaborator" is your own multifaceted self.)
3. **Experimental** — Ideas are tested, not just planned. Failure is feedback, not defeat.
4. **Iterative** — You revisit and refine. The process loops back on itself by design.
5. **Optimistic** — Design Thinking assumes that better solutions are always possible.


---
### The Five Phases: Empathize → Define → Ideate → Prototype → Test
---

Let us walk through each phase briefly. You will spend an entire module in each of these phases later in the course — this is just an orientation.

**Phase 1: Empathize**
This is the research phase. In traditional design, this means interviewing users and observing their behavior. In this course, it means turning that inquiry inward — studying yourself. Who are you? What do you value? What energizes you? What are your strengths and your growing edges?

**Phase 2: Define**
This is the clarity phase. Designers synthesize everything they learned in the Empathy phase into a clear problem statement. You will synthesize your self-knowledge into a focused academic direction — a degree plan with purpose behind it.

**Phase 3: Ideate**
This is the creativity phase. Designers generate as many ideas as possible without filtering. You will explore career possibilities broadly and boldly — before narrowing down.

**Phase 4: Prototype**
This is the building phase. Designers create rough, testable versions of their ideas. You will build a concrete career-degree roadmap — a plan you can actually act on.

**Phase 5: Test**
This is the feedback phase. Designers put their prototypes in front of real users and learn from the results. You will stress-test your plan, anticipate obstacles, and build resilience into your roadmap.

---
###  Design Thinking vs. Traditional Problem-Solving
---

Most of the decision-making you have been taught follows a linear path: identify the problem → research options → choose the best one → execute. This works well for problems that are well-defined and stable.

But choosing a career and designing a degree are not well-defined, stable problems. They are what designers call **wicked problems** — complex, shifting, and deeply personal. There is no single right answer. The conditions change. Your understanding of yourself changes. A linear approach to wicked problems tends to produce frustration, paralysis, or decisions you later regret.

| Traditional Problem-Solving | Design Thinking |
|----------------------------|-----------------|
| Start with a solution in mind | Start with understanding |
| Linear, step-by-step | Iterative, cyclical |
| Avoid failure | Use failure as data |
| One right answer | Multiple valid possibilities |
| Efficiency-focused | Human-centered |
| Locks in early | Stays flexible |

Design Thinking does not eliminate uncertainty. It teaches you to work *with* uncertainty — which is exactly the skill you need right now.

---
### Real-World Examples of Design Thinking in Action
---

**Example 1: IDEO and the Shopping Cart**
In a famous case study, IDEO was given five days to redesign the shopping cart. Rather than immediately improving the cart's mechanics, their team spent the first days in stores, observing shoppers, talking to parents with children, and interviewing store employees. What they built came directly from those human insights. The result was not just a better cart — it was a cart designed around real human behavior.

**Example 2: Stanford d.school and the Birth Kit**
Design Thinking teams worked with healthcare providers in developing countries to redesign the birth kit — a package of supplies for safe deliveries. By spending time with midwives and mothers, they discovered that the existing kit contained items that were never used and was missing items that were desperately needed. A solution built on research, not assumptions, saved lives.

**Example 3: Your Life**
Every time you have solved a real problem by paying attention to what was actually happening — rather than what you assumed was happening — you were practicing a version of Design Thinking. This course simply gives you a structured, repeatable way to do it.



# The Freshman Transition

---
### From High School to College: What Changes and What Stays the Same
---

The shift from high school to college is one of the largest environmental changes most people experience in early adulthood — and it happens fast. Understanding what is different will help you navigate it with intention rather than just reacting.

**What changes:**

| High School | College |
|-------------|---------|
| Structured schedule set by others | You build your own schedule |
| Teachers track your progress | Professors expect you to manage yourself |
| Assignments are frequent and small | Fewer, larger, higher-stakes assessments |
| Parents often monitor deadlines | You are responsible for your own deadlines |
| Social circle is largely assigned | You must build community intentionally |
| Path is mostly predetermined | Path requires active, ongoing decisions |

**What stays the same:**
- The habits you built — good and bad — come with you.
- The way you respond to stress, feedback, and uncertainty follows you.
- Your curiosity, when you choose to engage it, is still your greatest asset.

The transition is not just academic. It is psychological. Many students are surprised to find that the emotional adjustment is harder than the academic one. That is normal. And that is exactly what this course is designed to help you navigate.

### The Myth of "Having It All Figured Out"
---

Here is something no one tells you often enough: **most of your classmates do not know what they want to do with their lives either.**

The student who declared a major before orientation is not necessarily more certain than you — they may simply be more comfortable performing certainty. The student who says they want to be a doctor may change directions by sophomore year. The student who seems to have a five-year plan may have copied it from a parent's expectation rather than their own reflection.

Uncertainty at this stage is not a problem to be solved. It is **the appropriate condition** for someone who is just beginning to understand who they are and what the world offers.

Design Thinking does not ask you to pretend you have the answers. It asks you to get curious about the questions. That is a fundamentally different — and more honest — starting place.

> *"The goal is not certainty. The goal is a better question."*

### Growth Mindset vs. Fixed Mindset — Carol Dweck's Framework
---

Psychologist Carol Dweck spent decades studying how people respond to challenges, failure, and effort. Her research produced one of the most important frameworks in modern education: the concept of **mindset**.

**Fixed Mindset** — the belief that your abilities, intelligence, and talents are fixed traits. You either have them or you do not. Challenges are threatening. Failure is evidence of inadequacy. Effort feels pointless if you are "not good" at something.

**Growth Mindset** — the belief that your abilities can be developed through dedication, learning, and persistence. Challenges are opportunities. Failure is feedback. Effort is the mechanism of improvement.

| Fixed Mindset | Growth Mindset |
|---------------|----------------|
| "I'm not good at this." | "I'm not good at this *yet*." |
| Avoids challenges | Embraces challenges |
| Gives up when it gets hard | Persists through difficulty |
| Sees effort as weakness | Sees effort as the path |
| Ignores useful feedback | Learns from feedback |
| Threatened by others' success | Inspired by others' success |

The relevance to this course is direct: **designing your degree and career requires a growth mindset.** You will encounter assessments that reveal uncomfortable truths. You will set goals that feel uncertain. You will be asked to explore possibilities that feel unfamiliar. A fixed mindset will cause you to retreat from that process. A growth mindset will allow you to move through it.

The good news: mindset is not fixed. It can change. And becoming aware of your current default is the first step.

###  Navigating College Resources
---

One of the most consistent findings in college success research is this: **students who use campus resources perform better and persist longer.** Yet most students — especially freshmen — underutilize the resources available to them, often because they do not know what exists or feel uncertain about reaching out.

Key resources to know from day one:

| Resource | What It Does | When to Use It |
|----------|-------------|----------------|
| **Academic Advisor** | Helps you navigate degree requirements, course selection, and academic planning | Before each registration period — and anytime you have questions |
| **Career Services** | Helps with career exploration, resume writing, internship searches, and employer connections | Earlier than you think — even in your first year |
| **Tutoring / Academic Support Center** | Free academic assistance in most subjects | The moment a class feels difficult — do not wait |
| **Counseling Services** | Mental health support and stress management | Anytime — this is not a last resort, it is a resource |
| **Financial Aid Office** | Scholarship information, aid appeals, financial planning | Anytime your financial situation changes |
| **CECS Advising Office** | Specialized advising for CECS students and interdisciplinary degree planning | Each semester, and whenever you have questions about your CECS path |








# Introduction to Your Personal Design Portfolio

---
### What Is a Design Portfolio and Why Are You Building One?
---

In the design world, a portfolio is not just a collection of finished work. It is a **record of thinking** — evidence of how a designer moved through problems, made decisions, and grew over time. The process matters as much as the product.

Your **Personal Design Portfolio** works the same way. It is not a resume. It is not a highlight reel. It is a living document that captures:

- Where you started
- What you discovered about yourself
- How your thinking evolved
- What decisions you made and why
- What your plan looks like — and how it changed

By the end of this course, your portfolio will contain  one for each module. Together, they will tell the story of how you designed your academic and professional future. It will be something you can return to, revise, and build on long after this course ends.

---
### Setting Up Your Personal Design Portfolio on GitHub
---
our **Personal Design Portfolio** is the central artifact of this course. It is a living document — not a one-time assignment — that you will build, revise, and expand across all five modules. By the end of the course, it will contain:

- Self-assessments and personal reflections
- Your values, interests, and strengths analysis
- Your degree plan and SMART goals
- Your career vision and RIASEC results
- Your Career-Degree Roadmap
- Evidence of your growth as a thinker and planner

Why **GitHub**? Because your portfolio should not just be a Word document that lives on your laptop. GitHub is a platform used by professionals across virtually every industry — not just technology — to build, share, and version-control their work. By hosting your portfolio on GitHub, you will:

- Create a **real, publicly shareable professional artifact** from day one.
- Learn a tool that is valued across industries.
- Practice **version control** — the ability to track how your thinking changes over time.
- Build something you can show to advisors, mentors, and future employers.

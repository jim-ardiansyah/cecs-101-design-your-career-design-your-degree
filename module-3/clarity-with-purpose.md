
# The Define Phase in Design Thinking

---
### 3.1.1 From Empathy to Clarity: What the Define Phase Accomplishes
---


In the world of design, empathy produces data. The Define Phase turns that data into a decision.

Consider what you gathered in Module 2:
- A ranked list of your core values
- An inventory of your genuine interests
- A Personal SWOT Matrix that named your strengths, weaknesses, opportunities, and threats
- A completed Empathy Map that captured how you think, feel, say, and do
- Insights from the Ikigai Framework and additional self-assessment tools

That is a significant amount of self-knowledge. But raw data, no matter how accurate, does not automatically produce clarity. You have to do the interpretive work — the work of asking: *What does all of this actually mean for the decisions I need to make right now?*

That is the core task of the Define Phase.

In design practice, this phase produces what is called a **Point of View (POV) Statement** — a clear, focused articulation of the problem you are solving. A well-crafted POV statement does three things:

1. **Centers the user** — in your case, that user is you.
2. **Names a meaningful need** — what do you actually need from your education?
3. **Reveals an insight** — what surprising or important thing did you learn about yourself that should shape your direction?

A weak POV statement sounds like this:
> *"I need to pick a major."*

A strong POV statement sounds like this:
> *"I am a first-generation college student who values creative problem-solving and connection to my community. I need a degree path that combines technical skills with human-centered work, because I have discovered that I lose motivation when I cannot see how my work directly impacts real people."*

Notice the difference. The second statement is grounded in self-knowledge. It names a real need and reveals an insight. It gives you something to design *toward*.

---
### Why Clarity Matters Before You Build
---

Many students skip the Define Phase entirely — not because they are careless, but because the pressure to have answers is immediate. Advisors ask about your major. Parents ask about your career plan. The registrar asks which courses to enroll in. Everyone seems to want clarity from you before you feel ready to provide it.

The temptation is to declare something — anything — just to relieve that pressure.

This is one of the most common and costly mistakes in early college planning.

When you declare a direction before you have clearly defined your needs and insights, you are essentially guessing. Sometimes you guess right. More often, you spend two or three semesters discovering that the guess was wrong — and by then, you have lost time, money, and momentum.

The Define Phase is not about slowing down. It is about slowing down *just enough* to make sure you are moving in the right direction before you accelerate.

Think of it this way: A GPS that does not know your destination cannot give you directions — it can only show you the road. The Define Phase is how you enter your destination into the GPS.

---
### How to Use What You Learned in Module 2
---

Before writing your POV Statement, take time to revisit your Module 2 work. Look for patterns and convergences across your different assessments. Specifically, ask yourself:

**From your Values Clarification:**
- What were your top 5 values?
- Do your current academic interests align with those values?

**From your Interest Inventory:**
- What subjects, activities, or problems consistently appear on your list?
- Are there any surprising items — things you enjoy but have never considered seriously?

**From your Personal SWOT Matrix:**
- What strengths can you build an academic direction around?
- What opportunities exist at CECS or your institution that align with who you are?

**From your Empathy Map and Ikigai:**
- What do you care deeply about?
- What did you learn that the world needs that you are also good at?
- Where do your passion and potential converge?

The goal is not to use all of this data simultaneously — that would be overwhelming. The goal is to look for the **three or four themes** that appear consistently across multiple assessments. Those recurring themes are your signal. They are telling you something reliable about who you are and what kind of work will sustain your energy and motivation.


# Understanding Your Degree Options

---
### How College Degrees Are Structured
---


Before you can make informed decisions about your academic direction, you need to understand how a college degree is actually built. Many students arrive at college without a clear picture of this — and that gap leads to confusion about requirements, wasted elective credits, and missed opportunities.

Here is the essential structure of a typical four-year bachelor's degree:

**Total Credits Required:** Most bachelor's degrees require between **120 and 128 credit hours** to complete.

These credits are divided into three main categories:

| Category | Typical Credit Range | What It Includes |
|----------|---------------------|-----------------|
| **General Education (Core)** | 30–45 credits | Required foundational courses: writing, math, science, humanities, social sciences |
| **Major Requirements** | 30–60 credits | Courses specific to your declared major or concentration |
| **Electives** | 20–40 credits | Free or restricted electives — courses you choose based on interest |

Understanding this structure matters because it reveals something important: **you have more flexibility than you think.** Your elective credits, in particular, are a powerful tool. Used intentionally, they can build a minor, develop a second area of expertise, or explore career-relevant skills that your major does not cover.

---
### Majors, Minors, Concentrations, and Certificates
---

Students often use these terms interchangeably, but they mean different things:

**Major** — Your primary field of study. It is the core academic identity of your degree and typically requires the most credits. Your transcript and diploma will identify your major.

**Minor** — A secondary field of study that requires fewer credits than a major (typically 15–18 credits). A minor does not appear on your diploma but does appear on your transcript. It signals depth in a second area.

**Concentration** — A specialized focus area *within* a major. For example, a Psychology major might have a concentration in Clinical Psychology or Industrial-Organizational Psychology.

**Certificate** — A credential that demonstrates competency in a specific skill set, often completed in 12–18 credits. Certificates are increasingly valued by employers, particularly in technical and professional fields.

**Why does this matter?** Because your degree is not just your major. A thoughtful student uses all available options — major, minor, elective courses, certificates — to build a degree that is more than the sum of its parts.

---
### CECS and Interdisciplinary Degrees
---

The College of Emerging and Collaborative Studies (CECS) was created with a specific philosophy: the most interesting and important problems in the world do not fit neatly into a single discipline.

Climate change is not just a science problem — it is a policy problem, a design problem, a communication problem, and an economics problem. Healthcare is not just a medicine problem — it is a technology problem, an ethics problem, a cultural problem, and a systems problem.

CECS encourages — and in some cases allows — students to design **interdisciplinary degree paths** that draw from multiple fields. This means you are not always forced to choose between two things you love. In some cases, you can pursue both, in a structured and intentional way.

This flexibility is powerful. But it also requires *more* intentionality from you, not less. An interdisciplinary degree that is assembled without purpose is just a collection of random courses. An interdisciplinary degree built around a clear POV Statement and a coherent career direction is a competitive and compelling credential.

---
### 3.2.4 Declared vs. Undecided: Both Are Valid Starting Points
---

There is significant stigma in college culture around being "undecided." Students feel pressure to have a major declared — ideally one that sounds impressive — as quickly as possible. This pressure comes from everywhere: family, peers, social media, and sometimes even well-meaning advisors.

Let us address this directly: **being undecided is not the same as being lost.**

A student who declares a major impulsively to relieve social pressure and then spends two years in courses they do not care about is in a much more difficult position than a student who takes one additional semester to explore intentionally before committing.

That said, "undecided" should not be passive. The Design Thinking approach transforms "undecided" from a state of uncertainty into a state of *active inquiry*. You are not drifting — you are exploring with a method and a framework.

Whether you arrive at this module with a major in mind or without one, your task is the same: examine your options carefully, connect them to your self-knowledge, and make the most informed decision you can with the information you currently have. Then remain open to refining that decision as you learn more.

---
### Working with Your Academic Advisor
---

Your academic advisor is one of the most underutilized resources in college. Many students see their advisor once a semester — for course registration — and treat the meeting as a bureaucratic formality.

This is a missed opportunity.

A well-prepared advising meeting is a strategic conversation. Your advisor knows the degree requirements, the course sequencing, the policy exceptions, the opportunities, and the pitfalls that you do not yet know about. But they can only help you as much as you help them — by showing up prepared with questions, ideas, and the kind of self-knowledge you are building in this course.

**What to bring to an advising meeting:**
- Your POV Statement (from Section 3.1)
- A list of 2–3 majors or pathways you are considering
- Specific questions about requirements, course sequencing, or opportunities
- Your Draft Degree Plan (from Section 3.5 — bring it even in draft form)

**Questions worth asking your advisor:**
- *What do students with this major typically do after graduation?*
- *Are there experiential learning requirements — internships, practicums, fieldwork?*
- *What elective courses do you see students in this field most benefit from?*
- *Are there any interdisciplinary options that might be a good fit for my interests?*
- *What should I be doing in my first year beyond coursework?*



# Connecting Career Interests to Your Degree

---
### Reverse Engineering Your Degree
---

Most students think about degree planning in one direction: *I will take the required courses, and then I will figure out what to do with my degree afterward.*

Design Thinking suggests a different approach — one that is more strategic and more aligned with how the professional world actually works.

Instead of starting with courses and ending at a career, **start with the career and work backward to the courses.**

This approach is called *reverse engineering your degree*, and it works like this:

1. Identify a career direction or professional role that genuinely interests you.
2. Research what skills, knowledge, and experiences people in that role actually need.
3. Identify which courses, majors, minors, and extracurricular experiences develop those skills.
4. Build your degree plan to include those courses intentionally — not randomly.

This does not mean you become rigidly pre-professional at age 18. Liberal education has tremendous value, and exploration is important. But it does mean that you are making intentional choices with your electives and course selections rather than filling credit hours with whatever is convenient.

---
### Researching Career Pathways
---


Effective career research goes beyond a basic Google search of "what can I do with a psychology degree." It requires you to look at the actual landscape of a profession — who works in it, what they do day to day, how they got there, and what skills they needed.

Several high-quality resources support this kind of research:

**O*NET Online (onetonline.org)** — A comprehensive database of occupations maintained by the U.S. Department of Labor. For any career, O*NET provides detailed information on required tasks, skills, knowledge areas, typical education levels, and salary data.

**Bureau of Labor Statistics Occupational Outlook Handbook (bls.gov/ooh)** — Provides job growth projections, median salaries, and educational requirements for hundreds of occupations.

**LinkedIn** — Search for professionals in roles that interest you. Look at their educational backgrounds and career trajectories. This shows you real-world paths, not idealized ones.

**Informational Interviews** — Conversations with professionals currently working in fields you are considering. These are among the most valuable career research tools available, and they are almost always free. Most professionals are willing to speak briefly with a student who reaches out respectfully and comes prepared with thoughtful questions.

---
### The T-Shaped Professional
---


One of the most useful frameworks for thinking about how to build your skills in college is the concept of the **T-Shaped Professional**.

Imagine the letter T:
- The **vertical bar** represents *depth* — deep expertise in one specific area (typically your major).
- The **horizontal bar** represents *breadth* — a range of complementary skills and knowledge across multiple areas.

Employers — and graduate schools — increasingly value T-shaped individuals because they can work effectively within their area of expertise *and* collaborate across disciplines, communicate with diverse audiences, and adapt to changing environments.

For you, this framework has direct implications for how you design your degree:

| Vertical Bar (Depth) | Horizontal Bar (Breadth) |
|----------------------|--------------------------|
| Your major | Minors, certificates, electives |
| Core technical skills in your field | Communication, data literacy, project management |
| Specialized knowledge | Cultural competency, collaboration, systems thinking |

Your goal in degree planning is not just to satisfy major requirements. It is to build both dimensions of the T — deliberately and strategically.



# Goal Setting with the SMART Framework

---
### 3.4.1 Why Most Goals Fail
---

Goals fail for a remarkably consistent set of reasons — and almost none of them are about motivation or work ethic.

Most goals fail because they are too vague to act on.

Consider the difference between these two statements:

- *"I want to do well in college."*
- *"I will earn a GPA of 3.3 or higher in my first semester by attending every class, completing all readings before lectures, and visiting my professors' office hours at least once per course."*

The first statement is a wish. The second is a goal. The difference is specificity — and specificity is what makes a goal actionable rather than aspirational.

The most widely used framework for writing effective goals is the **SMART framework**, originally developed for business planning but now applied in education, healthcare, personal development, and career planning. In this section, you will learn to use it rigorously.

---
### The SMART Framework — Defined
---

Each letter in SMART represents a quality that every well-written goal should have:

| Letter | Quality | What It Means |
|--------|---------|---------------|
| **S** | Specific | The goal is clearly defined — you know exactly what you are trying to achieve |
| **M** | Measurable | You can track progress and know definitively when the goal is met |
| **A** | Achievable | The goal is challenging but realistic given your current resources and circumstances |
| **R** | Relevant | The goal aligns with your values, interests, and larger direction |
| **T** | Time-Bound | The goal has a clear deadline or timeframe |

Let us apply this framework to a real example:

**Weak goal:** *"I want to get better at writing."*

**SMART version:**
> *"By the end of my first semester, I will complete the Writing Center's three-part workshop series and submit at least two essays for peer review through the Writing Center, with the goal of increasing my written communication score on my next evaluated assignment by at least one letter grade."*

- **Specific:** Writing Center workshops + peer review
- **Measurable:** Three workshops, two essays, one letter grade improvement
- **Achievable:** Writing Center services are free and available
- **Relevant:** Strong writing is essential for most career paths and academic success
- **Time-Bound:** By the end of the first semester

---
### Types of Goals: Academic, Career, and Personal Development
---

For the purposes of this course, you will write goals in three categories:

**Academic Goals** — Related to your performance, habits, and progress as a student.
- GPA targets
- Study habits and time management practices
- Specific course performance
- Relationships with professors and mentors

**Career Goals** — Related to your professional development and career preparation.
- Internship and job shadowing opportunities
- Skill development
- Networking and professional connections
- Certifications or credentials

**Personal Development Goals** — Related to your growth as a person and a learner.
- Leadership and involvement on campus
- Health and well-being practices
- Community engagement
- Cross-cultural competency

You do not need to have fully formed goals in every category right now. What matters is that your goals connect to your POV Statement — they should be moving you in the direction you defined.

---
### Short-Term vs. Long-Term Goals: Building a Goal Hierarchy
---

Effective goal-setting recognizes that goals exist at different time horizons and that short-term goals should serve long-term goals.

Think of it as a goal hierarchy:

```
LONG-TERM GOAL (3–5 years)
    └── MID-TERM GOAL (1–2 years)
            └── SHORT-TERM GOAL (This semester)
                    └── IMMEDIATE ACTION (This week)
```

**Example:**

- **Long-term (5 years):** Secure a position as a public health researcher at a nonprofit or government agency.
- **Mid-term (2 years):** Complete a summer internship in a public health setting and establish a relationship with a faculty research mentor.
- **Short-term (this semester):** Earn a B+ or higher in BIOL 101 and attend the Campus Health Research Symposium.
- **Immediate action:** Email the Biology department advisor this week to ask about research assistant opportunities for sophomores.

Notice how each level serves the one above it. Short-term goals are not arbitrary — they are chosen because they directly advance the mid-term goal, which advances the long-term goal.


# Building Your Draft Degree Plan

---
### What a Degree Plan Is — and Why "Draft" Is the Right Word
---

A degree plan is a semester-by-semester roadmap of the courses you intend to take from your first semester through graduation. It maps out which requirements you will fulfill when, how your electives will be used, and how you will sequence your academic journey.

The word *draft* is intentional and important.

Your degree plan will change. It will change when you discover a new interest, when a course is not offered in a particular semester, when you add or change a major, when life circumstances shift your timeline, or when an advisor suggests a better sequencing. A degree plan is not a contract — it is a living document, a working tool that gets refined over time.

What matters right now is that you build a first draft that is **intentional** — grounded in your POV Statement, informed by your career research, and organized around your SMART Goals. A thoughtful draft is infinitely more useful than no plan at all.

---
### How to Read a Degree Audit
---

Most institutions provide students with a **degree audit** — a tool that shows which requirements you have fulfilled and which remain. It is typically accessible through your student portal and automatically updates as you complete courses.

A degree audit is organized by category:
- **General Education Requirements** — which core courses you need and which you have completed
- **Major Requirements** — specific courses required for your declared major
- **Minor Requirements** (if applicable)
- **Elective Credits** — how many free or restricted electives remain
- **Total Credits** — your progress toward the 120+ credits required for graduation

Learning to read your degree audit early — in your first semester — gives you a significant advantage. Students who do not engage with it often discover missed requirements late in their college career, when there is little time to correct them.

> 💡 **Action:** Log into your student portal this week and locate your degree audit. If you have not declared a major, your audit will show general education requirements only — which is still useful information.

---
### Principles for Building a Strong Degree Plan
---


As you draft your four-year plan, keep the following principles in mind:

**1. Sequence matters.**
Some courses have prerequisites. You cannot take Organic Chemistry before General Chemistry. Map prerequisites carefully so you are not blocked in a later semester.

**2. Balance your load.**
Avoid stacking all of your most difficult courses in one semester. A balanced semester includes a mix of challenging required courses and lighter or more personally engaging electives.

**3. Use electives with intention.**
Elective credits are not filler. They are your opportunity to build the horizontal bar of your T-Shaped profile — to develop skills and knowledge that complement your major and strengthen your career readiness.

**4. Build in flexibility.**
Leave room in your later semesters for courses you have not discovered yet. Your interests will evolve, and your plan should be able to accommodate that evolution without derailing your timeline.

**5. Plan for experiences, not just courses.**
A degree plan should include not only courses but also significant experiential milestones: internships, research assistantships, study abroad semesters, capstone projects, and leadership roles. These experiences often matter as much as — or more than — your GPA.

But the work is not complete. The Define Phase is not a one-time event. Your POV Statement will evolve. Your degree plan will change. Your goals will be achieved, refined, or replaced. This is not failure — this is Design Thinking working as it should.

What you have built in this module is a **baseline** — an informed, intentional starting point from which to navigate the rest of your undergraduate experience.
